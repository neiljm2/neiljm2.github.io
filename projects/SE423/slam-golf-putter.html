Mapping & Exploration

Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) was implemented by fusing dead reckoning odometry with LIDAR range scanning. Localized pose estimation (absolute coordinates $x_{abs}$, $y_{abs}$ in feet and orientation $\theta$ in radians) was tracked by integrating optical wheel encoder values and IMU gyro outputs at a high frequency:

$$x_{t} = x_{t-1} + \cos(\theta_{t}) \cdot V_{avg} \cdot dt$$ $$y_{t} = y_{t-1} + \sin(\theta_{t}) \cdot V_{avg} \cdot dt$$

To map the arena, the robot initially executed a proportional Right Wall Following algorithm (rightWallFollow()) during State 0. This autonomous wall-tracing routine allowed the LiDAR sensor to sweep the boundaries and construct the outer map outline.

The occupancy grid was stored in a 2D integer array of size $101 \times 101$ cells (map[101][101]). Coordinate transformations mapped absolute coordinates $(x_{abs}, y_{abs})$ into discrete grid indices $(x_{idx}, y_{idx})$:

$$x_{idx} = \text{floor}\left(x_{abs} \cdot 3\right) + 50$$ $$y_{idx} = -\text{floor}\left(y_{abs} \cdot 3\right) + 50$$

The scaling factor of 3 (UNIT_MAP1_LEN_INV) yields a resolution of 4 inches per grid cell, while the offset of 50 shifts the absolute origin $(0, 0)$ to the center of the array.

When range measurements identified an obstacle, the corresponding cell was incremented by +9 (capping at +30). To eliminate transient "ghost" obstacles, a ray-clearing function (clearPath()) ran Bresenham's line algorithm along the line-of-sight between the robot's current cell and the obstacle hit. Cells traversed by the beam were decremented by -4 (flooring at -30). If a cell's confidence value flipped below 0, it was dynamically cleared.

Perception of dynamic target items was handled by a front-mounted camera. The camera used color-based blob detection to locate colorful golf balls and AprilTag detection to track the scoring goal. When either target was spotted within the camera's field of view, the control loop temporarily stopped forward translation ($V_{ref} = 0.0$) and turned the chassis to center the target in the image frame. Once centered, the robot calculated the absolute range to the target:

$$d_{goal} = |tag_z \cdot 0.00425| + \frac{3.5}{12}$$ $$d_{ball} = \left(264.2167 - 6.144 \cdot Row + 0.0523 \cdot Row^2 - 0.00015432 \cdot Row^3\right) \cdot 0.08333$$

Using this distance and the robot's current pose, the system resolved the absolute coordinates:

$$x_{target} = x_{abs} + d_{target} \cdot \cos(\theta)$$ $$y_{target} = y_{abs} + d_{target} \cdot \sin(\theta)$$

Once these coordinates were resolved, the target items were written into the occupancy grid map to dynamically define navigation waypoints.

To optimize serial bandwidth, coordinate updates were only queued when their occupancy state sign flipped. These events were pushed to a custom RingBuffer LVbuffer and popped (rb_pop()) to be sent over serial to LabVIEW. Because the updates were queued and transmitted sequentially at a throttled low frequency, the LabVIEW visualization in the video exhibits a distinct lag/delay behind the robot's real-time movement.

Gradient Descent Navigation

Autonomous navigation was driven by a virtual Potential Field Path Planner implementing gradient descent. During State 1 (exploration), State 10 (line-up), and State 21 (kicking approach), the controller continuously calculated attractive forces pulling the robot toward targeted waypoints and repulsive forces pushing it away from mapped obstacle regions.

In this path-planning framework, the targeted coordinates act as a well (the lowest potential valley, creating a strong attractive pull). Conversely, walls, obstacles, and the golf ball itself act as peaks (regions of maximum potential and high cost). The robot will then "roll down the hill", or travel on a path that pushes it from regions of high potential to regions of low potential.

2D Gradient Descent Simulation. The wells (valleys) represent target positions, while the peaks represent obstacles, walls, and the golf ball to prevent collisions. Source: Wikipedia


The controller solved the cumulative potential gradient at the robot's current position to determine the desired velocity vectors $V_x$ and $V_y$:

$$V_x = k_{goal} \cdot (x_{target} - x_{robot}) + \sum \frac{k_{obs}}{2 \cdot d_{obs}^4} \cdot dx_{obs} + \frac{k_{ball}}{2 \cdot d_{ball}^4} \cdot dx_{ball}$$ $$V_y = k_{goal} \cdot (y_{target} - y_{robot}) + \sum \frac{k_{obs}}{2 \cdot d_{obs}^4} \cdot dy_{obs} + \frac{k_{ball}}{2 \cdot d_{ball}^4} \cdot dy_{ball}$$

By adjusting the velocity command magnitude ($V_{ref}$) and heading rate command ($\omega_{turn}$) to descend this gradient, the robot steered smooth trajectories around obstacles and the ball:

$$\theta_{desired} = \text{atan2}(-V_y, V_x)$$ $$\omega_{turn} = -k_{turn} \cdot (\theta_{desired} - \theta_{robot})$$

Outcome

The robot was able to successfully navigate around obstacles, find the golf ball, and putt it into the cup. While the autonomous navigation and aiming worked reliably, the launch mechanism seemed to have a lot of directional variation, leading to unreliable short range putts. That said, I'm very proud of the navigation and SLAM algorithms, and I learned a lot about these algorithms as well as low level control, and I had a great time doing it.
